Reporting Security Issues
Reporting Security Issues
Plex Bug Bounty Policy
At Plex, protecting our users’ media, accounts, and privacy is core to everything we build. We invite security researchers of all backgrounds to help us find and fix vulnerabilities before they can be exploited. If you’ve found a security issue in a Plex product or service, we want to hear from you. We’re committed to working with you fairly, transparently, and in good faith.
This page explains what’s in scope, what we’re looking for, what won’t qualify, how to test responsibly, and how to submit a report.
Program Scope
Please review the following carefully before you begin testing or submit a report. Reports that fall outside the defined scope, or that match a listed out-of-scope category, generally will not be eligible for a reward.
In-Scope Assets
Plex web applications: app.plex.tv, watch.plex.tv, and other first-party web properties operated by Plex.
Plex-owned domains: publicly accessible services under *.plex.tv, *.plexapp.com, or any of their subdomains that are operated by Plex.
Plex Media Server (PMS): the Plex Media Server application and the official plexinc/pms-docker container, in their current released form.
Plex client applications: official Plex mobile, TV, desktop, and console apps that are actively maintained.
Plex APIs: first-party APIs served from Plex-operated infrastructure.
Out-of-Scope Assets
Any service hosted or operated by a third party, unless you can demonstrate a concrete impact on Plex-operated assets or user data.
A user’s own self-hosted Plex Media Server instance, or third-party apps built on top of Plex (e.g., Tautulli, “*arr” tools). These are not Plex-operated infrastructure.
Legacy or unmaintained applications that are no longer actively developed (for example, older TV or Alexa integrations).
Community and forum platforms hosted on third-party services (e.g., forums.plex.tv, which runs on Discourse). Please report those through the hosting provider’s own security program.
Vulnerabilities We’re Interested In
We especially want to hear about issues with real, demonstrable security impact, including:
Remote Code Execution (RCE) in Plex client applications, Plex Media Server, or cloud infrastructure
Local privilege escalation in Plex desktop clients, installers, or background/updater services (e.g., code execution as SYSTEM or root)
Privilege escalation and authentication/authorization bypass
Entitlement or purchase-integrity bypass, unlocking Plex Pass or premium features without payment, or defeating in-app-purchase / StoreKit receipt validation
Cross-account data access / IDOR affecting other users’ private data
Plex Media Server server-side flaws, including authorization or library-sharing bypass and cross-library data access, path traversal / arbitrary file read, and disclosure of owner tokens or absolute file paths to shared or managed (Plex Home) users
Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) with genuine cross-user impact (stored or reflected)
Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF) with a meaningful state-changing effect
SQL injection and other server-side injection
Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) that reaches internal services, cloud metadata, or private networks
Security misconfigurations that expose sensitive data or credentials
Exposure of secrets, credentials, or sensitive personal data caused by a Plex-side flaw
Exploitable issues on our marketing and WordPress properties. For example, stored or reflected XSS, web cache poisoning, injection, access-control flaws, or exposure of sensitive data
Email/DNS/TLS misconfigurations or regressions. e.g.: a record that is missing, broken, expired, or downgraded from where it should be (SPF, DKIM, DMARC, DNSSEC, CAA, MTA-STS, TLS chain, etc.) and that creates real, demonstrable risk.
When you report, please tell us the concrete impact and a realistic attack scenario.
Vulnerabilities That Are Out-of-Scope
The categories below are the ones we receive most often that do not qualify. In almost every case, they reflect intended behavior, a known and accepted trade-off, an issue with no crossing of a security/trust boundary, or scanner output with no demonstrated impact. Reports that fall squarely into these categories will typically be closed as non-qualifying without a reward. If you believe you have a genuine exception with real impact, tell us specifically how it crosses a trust boundary and affects another user.
Session, token & authentication lifecycle
Sessions or tokens not automatically invalidated after logout, password change, email change, disabling 2FA, or revoking a linked provider. This is intended. Users who want to terminate other sessions can use the “sign out connected devices” option. Revoking all authentications on every account change is deliberately not our default.
Tokens (e.g., X-Plex-Token) appearing in URLs / GET requests over HTTPS. Tokens are obfuscated in logs and are not interceptable over TLS. We’ll only consider this with a demonstrated real interception path.
Access/auth tokens stored in browser localStorage absent a separately-demonstrated vulnerability that reads them.
Long-lived or long-idle sessions remaining valid. Session length is an intentional usability trade-off.
2FA / TOTP / recovery codes
TOTP codes being valid on multiple devices, or reusable within their time window. This is how time-based OTP works by design.
2FA recovery/backup codes remaining valid across devices, after a password change, or “indefinitely.” Backup codes are meant to keep working.
Email verification not being required to enable 2FA, or “bypassing” email verification. Email verification is a recovery fallback, not a security control, and requiring it here would be false security (control of the inbox already yields an emailed code).
No re-authentication prompt before sensitive actions (linking a provider, changing a payment method, etc.) when the user is already authenticated.
Rate limiting & anti-automation
Rate-limit / brute-force / OTP-resend “bypasses”, including via IP rotation, VPN/proxy, X-Forwarded-For spoofing, browser-fingerprint changes, multi-account rotation, concurrent requests, or logout/login token cycling. We do not reward rate-limiting evasion.
Missing rate limiting as a standalone finding, including “email flooding” via forms, unless you can show concrete, high-impact abuse.
HTTP headers, redirects & CORS
Missing HTTP security headers (HSTS, CSP, CORP, COOP, X-Permitted-Cross-Domain-Policies, X-Frame-Options, etc.) without a proven, exploitable impact.
Clickjacking on pages with no sensitive, state-changing action.
Open redirects and CORS “misconfigurations” on non-browser-facing (provider/admin/API) endpoints, or where no token, credential, or sensitive data is exposed.
Information disclosure with no sensitive data
env.js / JavaScript environment disclosure, exposed non-secret config files (e.g., .scrutinizer.yml), server-status/server-info pages, banners, version strings, and similar passive disclosures that reveal nothing sensitive.
Files that are intentionally public — ads.txt, security.txt, robots.txt, .well-known/ contents, .env.example templates, and similar. These are meant to be reachable and disclose nothing sensitive.
Secrets or credentials found in repositories, URLs, or archives that Plex does not own, or that Plex did not cause to be exposed/indexed.
Credentials from third-party breach or “combo” dumps (dark web, LeakRadar, VirusTotal, etc.). These are pre-compromised users, not necessarily a Plex-side vulnerability.
Infrastructure & report quality
Denial of Service (DoS/DDoS) and resource-exhaustion reports, and any testing that degrades availability.
Findings requiring a pre-existing compromise or trust-boundary crossing, e.g., “if you have root in the container you can do X,” physical access to an unlocked device, or a self-hosted PMS the reporter already controls.
PIN-protection “bypass.” The profile/parental PIN is a convenience control, not a security boundary.
Reports against CVEs already patched, or against infrastructure/CVEs that are not ours (e.g., managed database instances not exposed by Plex).
Self-XSS, content spoofing / text injection with no cross-user impact, and issues requiring the victim to already possess the attacker’s (or their own) credentials.
Output of automated scanners with no demonstrated impact, purely theoretical vulnerabilities with no proof of exploitation
Issues affecting only outdated or end-of-life browsers or operating systems.
Rewards
Rewards are discretionary and are based on the severity, impact, and quality of each report. Depending on the finding, a reward may take the form of monetary compensation, a lifetime Plex Pass, or other recognition. We use the following severity ladder to set expectations about how we triage and how significant an issue is. It is not a fixed price list.
Severity
What it looks like
Examples
Critical
Full system compromise or large-scale breach
Remote code execution, database access, admin-panel access, mass exfiltration of user data
High
Access to sensitive data or elevated privileges without full compromise
Authentication bypass, privilege escalation, significant cross-account data exposure, SSRF reaching internal services
Medium
Real impact requiring some user interaction or unusual conditions
Stored/reflected XSS with cross-user impact, meaningful CSRF, notable API authorization flaws
Low
Minor issues or limited-impact information disclosure
Low-impact info disclosure, misconfigurations with limited security consequence
A few things to know about eligibility:
Only previously unknown vulnerabilities qualify. Duplicates of an already-reported or already-known issue are not eligible.
A vulnerability must be reproducible by Plex engineers to be eligible for a reward.
Reports without clear security implications, or that rely on unrealistic attack scenarios, will not be rewarded.
Monetary rewards are paid via PayPal. Researchers located in U.S.-sanctioned countries or regions are not eligible to receive monetary rewards. A current list can be found on this US Treasury Department page.
Submission Guidelines
Write to our Security team at [email protected]. If you’d like to encrypt your email, please use our public PGP key.
Please send one report per vulnerability, and include:
A clear title and a description of the vulnerability and its impact.
Step-by-step reproduction instructions that our engineers can follow.
The affected product, platform, URL/endpoint, and app version where applicable.
A proof of concept with screenshots, a short video, request/response captures, or code snippets.
The realistic attack scenario: who can exploit it, what they need, and what they gain.
When you test:
Only access, modify, or exfiltrate your own data. Use test accounts you control.
If you accidentally access another user’s data, stop immediately and report it. Do not access, store, or share it further.
Respect user privacy and the stability of our systems.
Do not use techniques that degrade service for other users.
Keep vulnerabilities confidential until we’ve had a chance to investigate and remediate; please coordinate any public disclosure with us.
Rate Limits & Testing Constraints
To keep testing safe for our users and systems, please:
Limit automated traffic to no more than 10 requests per minute.
Avoid testing that generates excessive emails or notifications (no more than 5 per hour).
Limit login/authentication attempts to no more than 10 per hour.
Avoid any testing that could affect system availability or impact other users.
Our Commitment
We aim to acknowledge new reports promptly and to begin triage as quickly as we can.
We’ll keep you updated on the status of your report as we investigate and work toward a resolution.
We’ll let you know our disposition, including when we determine an issue is intended behavior, a known/accepted trade-off, or out of scope, and why.
Legal Safe Harbor
We support responsible security research and will not pursue legal action against researchers who act in good faith and comply with this policy. As long as your research is conducted within the scope and constraints described here, testing only your own accounts and data, avoiding privacy violations and service disruption, and giving us a reasonable opportunity to remediate before any disclosure, we consider your activity authorized.
